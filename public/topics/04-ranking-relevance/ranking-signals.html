Ranking Signals

Six categories of signals that determine search relevance

01 What is a Signal?

A ranking signal is any feature or measurement that contributes to determining how relevant a document is to a query. Modern search systems combine dozens of signals to produce a final ranking.

📡

Analogy: Think of ranking like a hiring committee. Each committee member (signal) evaluates the candidate (document) from a different angle — resume keywords, cultural fit, experience, references. The final hiring decision combines all perspectives with different weights.

$$\text{score}(d, q) = \sum_{i=1}^{N} w_i \cdot \text{signal}_i(d, q)$$

Each signal contributes its weight × value to the final score

02 Keyword Signals

The foundation of search — does the document contain the words the user typed?

BM25

The gold standard for keyword matching. Considers term frequency, document length normalization, and inverse document frequency.

$$\text{BM25} = \text{IDF} \times \frac{tf \cdot (k_1 + 1)}{tf + k_1 \cdot (1 - b + b \cdot \frac{|D|}{avgdl})}$$

Exact Match

Binary signal — does the document contain the exact query string? Critical for product search (SKU, model numbers) and navigational queries.

Query: "iPhone 15 Pro Max"
Exact: 1 if title = "iPhone 15 Pro Max"
Partial: 0 if title = "iPhone 15 Pro"

Phrase Match

A middle ground between exact match and bag-of-words BM25. Rewards documents where query terms appear close together and in order.

Example: Query "machine learning" should rank a document with "...advances in machine learning have..." above one with "...the machine was learning to..."

Implemented via phrase queries in Elasticsearch/Lucene or proximity scoring.

Keyword Signal Comparison

Signal Precision Recall Latency When to Use
BM25 Medium High ~1ms Default first-stage retrieval
Exact Match Very High Very Low <1ms SKU, model number, ID lookups
Phrase Match High Medium ~2ms Multi-word queries, named entities
Fuzzy Match Low Very High ~5ms Handling typos and misspellings
Layered Keyword Strategy

Production systems often layer keyword signals: first try exact match (instant, highest precision), then phrase match, then BM25 as fallback. Boost exact matches 3× over phrase matches and 5× over bag-of-words BM25 hits.

03 Semantic Signals

Understand meaning, not just words. Semantic signals capture conceptual similarity even when the exact terms don't match.

Bi-Encoder (Vector Search)

Encode query and document independently into dense vectors. Score = cosine similarity.

  • Speed: Fast — pre-computed doc embeddings, only encode query at runtime
  • Quality: Good — captures semantic similarity broadly
  • Best for: First-stage retrieval (top-100 candidates)

Cross-Encoder (Reranker)

Feed query + document together into a transformer. Produces a single relevance score.

  • Speed: Slow — must run for every query-doc pair
  • Quality: Excellent — captures nuanced interactions
  • Best for: Reranking top-20 candidates from first stage

Choosing the Right Model

For most use cases, start with a fast bi-encoder (MiniLM) for retrieval and add a cross-encoder reranker for the final top-20. The two-stage approach gives you both speed and quality — the bi-encoder casts a wide net, the cross-encoder picks the best fish.

04 Popularity Signals

What other users found useful is likely useful for you too. Popularity signals encode collective wisdom.

Click-Through Rate (CTR)

% of impressions that result in clicks. Position-normalized CTR accounts for the fact that position 1 always gets more clicks.

$$\text{nCTR}(d) = \frac{\text{clicks}(d)}{\text{impressions}(d) \times \text{pos\_bias}(\text{rank}(d))}$$

Purchase / Conversion Rate

For e-commerce: how often a product is purchased after being viewed. Stronger signal than clicks because it indicates true satisfaction.

Dwell Time

How long a user stays on the result page. Short dwell (<10s) suggests irrelevant result (pogo-sticking). Long dwell (>30s) suggests satisfied user.

Cold Start Problem

New documents have zero clicks. Use a smoothed estimator like (clicks + α) / (impressions + β) where α and β are small constants (Bayesian smoothing) to avoid biasing against new content.

Popularity Signal Decay

Raw click counts are biased toward old documents (more time to accumulate clicks). Use time-windowed popularity instead:

Rolling Window

Count clicks from the last N days only. Common windows: 7 days (volatile), 30 days (stable), 90 days (conservative).

Exponential Decay

Recent clicks count more than old ones:
$\text{score} = \sum_i e^{-\lambda \cdot t_i}$ where $t_i$ is days since click $i$.

05 Freshness Signals

For news, social media, and time-sensitive search, newer content is more relevant — even if older content has better keyword matches.

Recency

How recently the document was published or updated. Apply a decay function (linear, exponential, or Gaussian) to reduce scores over time.

Today: 1.0
1 week ago: 0.85
1 month ago: 0.45
1 year ago: 0.05

Trending Velocity

Rate of engagement growth. A document published yesterday with 1,000 views is trending more than one from last week with 5,000 views.

$$\text{velocity} = \frac{\text{views}_{\text{last 24h}}}{\text{views}_{\text{last 7d}} / 7}$$

When Freshness Hurts

Not all queries benefit from freshness. "Python tutorial" should return the best tutorial, not the newest one. Use query classification to detect time-sensitive queries ("election results 2024") vs. evergreen queries ("how to sort a list in Python").

06 Quality Signals

Not all content is created equal. Quality signals separate authoritative sources from thin content and spam.

User Ratings

Average star rating (1-5). Use Bayesian average to avoid "1 review, 5 stars" dominating:

$$\bar{R} = \frac{v \cdot R + m \cdot C}{v + m}$$

v = votes, R = avg rating, m = minimum votes, C = global avg

Spam Score

Probability that content is spam, keyword-stuffed, or AI-generated without review. Use as a negative signal to demote low-quality results.

  • Keyword density > 3% → suspicious
  • No outbound links → potential thin content
  • Duplicate content across pages → SEO spam

Content Depth

Longer, more detailed content tends to be more authoritative. But length alone isn't quality — combine with readability score, number of images/code blocks, and structured headings.

🏆

Analogy: Quality signals are like a restaurant health inspection. A restaurant might have great food (relevance), be popular (click signals), and be new (freshness) — but if the health inspector gives it a low score, you'd think twice. Quality signals protect users from spam, thin content, and unreliable sources.

07 Personal Signals

The same query can have different ideal results for different users. Personalization signals tailor rankings to individual preferences.

User History

What has this user clicked, purchased, or bookmarked before? Weight results from categories and brands the user has shown interest in.

// Boost score for preferred categories
for cat in user.recent_categories:
    if doc.category == cat:
        score *= 1.3

Collaborative Filtering

"Users similar to you also liked..." Find users with similar click/purchase patterns and boost results they engaged with.

// Item-based CF
sim_items = find_similar_items(
    user.purchase_history
)
for item in results:
    if item in sim_items:
        score *= 1.2
Privacy & Filter Bubbles

Personalization must be balanced with diversity. Over-personalized results create filter bubbles where users never see new categories. Blend personalized results with a "diversity slot" (e.g., every 5th result is from an unexplored category).

Personalization Maturity Levels

Level Signal Source Complexity Privacy Risk
L0: None Same results for everyone Zero None
L1: Segment User type (premium, free), locale Low Low
L2: History Click/purchase history, preferences Medium Medium
L3: Real-Time Session behavior, current context High High
L4: Collaborative Similar users' behavior patterns Very High High

Location & Context

Implicit signals from the user's environment:

Device

Mobile users prefer shorter content. Desktop users can handle data tables.

Location

Boost local results for queries with local intent ("restaurants", "plumber").

Time of Day

Morning searches may be informational, evening searches more transactional.

09 Building a Scorecard

How to combine signals into a final ranking score in production.

Step 1: Normalize Each Signal

Each signal operates on a different scale. Normalize to [0, 1] before combining:

Min-Max Scaling

$\text{norm}(x) = \frac{x - x_{\min}}{x_{\max} - x_{\min}}$

Simple, but sensitive to outliers.

Percentile Ranking

$\text{norm}(x) = \text{percentile}(x, \text{all values})$

Robust to outliers, preserves relative ordering.

Step 2: Weight and Sum

$$\text{final}(d) = \sum_{i=1}^{6} w_i \cdot \text{norm}(\text{signal}_i(d))$$

Where $\sum w_i = 1$ (weights are normalized to sum to 1)

Don't Over-Engineer

Start with 2–3 signals (keyword + popularity + freshness). Add more signals only when you can measure their impact. Each additional signal adds complexity and potential for unexpected interactions.

Example Scorecard

Here's how a production e-commerce scorecard might look:

{
  "query": "running shoes",
  "document": "Nike Air Zoom Pegasus 40",

    "bm25_title": 12.4 → norm: 0.82,
    "vector_sim": 0.89 → norm: 0.89,
    "click_rate": 0.15 → norm: 0.75,

}

$$0.25 \times 0.82 + 0.15 \times 0.89 + 0.20 \times 0.75 + 0.10 \times 0.92 + 0.20 \times 0.92 + 0.10 \times 0.70 = 0.833$$

10 Key Takeaway

The Bottom Line

No single signal is sufficient. Keyword signals catch what users type, semantic signals catch what they mean, popularity signals encode collective wisdom, freshness handles time-sensitivity, quality filters noise, and personalization tailors results. The art is in finding the right mix for your use case — and that mix will be different for e-commerce, news, knowledge bases, and social feeds.

Start Simple

  • BM25 + popularity as your baseline
  • Add vector search for semantic coverage
  • Measure before and after each signal

Scale Up

  • Add freshness for time-sensitive domains
  • Introduce personalization with guardrails
  • Train an LTR model to learn optimal weights
