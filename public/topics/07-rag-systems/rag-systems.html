01 What is RAG?

Large Language Models are powerful but fundamentally limited: they hallucinate facts, their training data has a cutoff date, and they cannot access your private data. Retrieval-Augmented Generation (RAG) solves all three problems by giving the LLM relevant context at query time.

📖

The Open-Book Exam Analogy: Without RAG, an LLM is taking a closed-book exam - it can only use what it memorized during training. With RAG, it's an open-book exam. The model gets to look up relevant information before answering. It doesn't need to memorize every fact - it just needs to know how to find and use the right information.

The Three Core Problems

Hallucination

LLMs generate plausible-sounding but factually incorrect information. They can't distinguish between what they know and what they don't.

Knowledge Cutoff

Training data stops at a point in time. GPT-4's training data ends in April 2024. Anything after that is invisible to the model.

No Private Data Access

LLMs know nothing about your company's internal docs, your customer data, or your proprietary knowledge base.

RAG in one sentence

$$\text{RAG} = \underbrace{\color{#6c63ff}{\text{Retrieve}}}_{\text{find relevant docs}} + \underbrace{\color{#00c9a7}{\text{Augment}}}_{\text{add to prompt}} + \underbrace{\color{#ffd93d}{\text{Generate}}}_{\text{LLM produces answer}}$$
Retrieve relevant documents, augment the prompt with them, then generate an answer

When to use RAG:

You need RAG when the answer depends on specific, potentially changing information: company docs, product manuals, legal contracts, research papers, support tickets. If the knowledge is static and broad (like general coding style), fine-tuning might work. If the knowledge is dynamic, specific, or private, RAG is the right choice.

Key Design Decisions

Chunk Size

Too small = missing context. Too large = diluted relevance + wasted tokens. Typical: 256–1024 tokens.

Embedding Model

Must match your domain. General models work for general text; specialized domains may need fine-tuning.

Retrieval Strategy

Dense only? Sparse (BM25)? Hybrid? Do you need re-ranking? Each adds latency but may add quality.

LLM Choice

Bigger models reason better but cost more. Context window limits matter. Streaming vs batch affects UX.

The critical insight:

A RAG system is only as good as its retrieval. If you retrieve irrelevant chunks, even GPT-4 will produce wrong answers. This is why Sections 3–7 of this masterclass focus heavily on the retrieval pipeline - it's the make-or-break component.

03 Chunking for RAG

Chunking is how you split your documents into pieces that get embedded and retrieved. It's deceptively important - bad chunking destroys retrieval quality regardless of how good your embedding model is.

✂️

Analogy: Imagine ripping pages out of a textbook and handing random torn fragments to someone taking an exam. If you rip mid-sentence, the fragment is useless. If you give them a clean, self-contained section, they can answer the question. Chunking is about finding the right places to "cut."

Why Chunking Matters in RAG

Context Window Limits

You can only feed so many tokens to the LLM. Each chunk takes space. Smaller chunks = more diverse sources. Larger chunks = more context per source.

Relevance Precision

Large chunks dilute relevance - only 2 sentences might be relevant in a 1000-token chunk. Small chunks focus relevance but may lack surrounding context.

Chunking Strategies

Strategy How It Works Pros Cons
Fixed-Size Split every N characters/tokens Simple, predictable chunk sizes Splits mid-sentence, mid-paragraph
Recursive Character Split by \n\n, then \n, then sentence, then word Respects document structure Still somewhat arbitrary
Sentence-Based Split at sentence boundaries Semantically clean boundaries Variable chunk sizes
Semantic Use embeddings to find topic shifts Best semantic coherence Expensive, complex, slow
Parent-Child Small chunks for retrieval, return parent chunk Precise retrieval + full context More complex indexing

Chunk Size Tradeoffs

256 tokens

High precision, low context. Great for fact lookup. Risk: missing surrounding explanation.

512 tokens

Balanced default. Good for most use cases. Usually contains 1–2 complete paragraphs.

1024 tokens

More context, lower precision. Good for complex explanations. Risk: diluted relevance.

Overlap Strategy

Overlapping chunks ensures information at chunk boundaries isn't lost. Typical overlap: 10–20% of chunk size.

$$\text{Chunks} = \left\lceil \frac{\text{doc\_length} - \text{overlap}}{\text{chunk\_size} - \text{overlap}} \right\rceil$$
Number of chunks produced from a document with overlap

04 Embeddings for RAG

Embeddings convert text into dense numerical vectors that capture semantic meaning. Two texts with similar meaning will have vectors close together in the embedding space. This is the foundation of dense retrieval.

$$\text{embed}: \text{string} \;\to\; \mathbb{R}^d \qquad \text{similarity}(q, d) = \frac{\mathbf{q} \cdot \mathbf{d}}{\|\mathbf{q}\| \cdot \|\mathbf{d}\|}$$
Text is mapped to a d-dimensional vector; similarity is measured by cosine distance

GTE-Qwen2-7B 3584 70.2 131,072 LLM-based embedding, SOTA open-source

Symmetric vs Asymmetric Models

Symmetric

Both sides are the same "type" of text. Used for finding similar documents, duplicate detection, clustering.

Example: "How to train a dog" ↔ "Dog training tips"

Asymmetric

Query is short, document is long. Used for search & RAG. Models like E5 use prefixes: query: for questions, passage: for documents.

Example: "What causes rain?" ↔ "Precipitation occurs when water vapor..."

Matryoshka Embeddings

🪆

Analogy: Like Russian nesting dolls, Matryoshka embeddings encode the most important information in the first dimensions. You can truncate a 3072-dim vector to 256 dims and still get useful (though less precise) similarity search. This lets you trade storage/speed for quality.

Dimension vs Quality Tradeoff (OpenAI text-embedding-3-large):
Dimensions Relative Quality Storage per Vector Use Case
256 ~93% 1 KB Fast prototyping, mobile
1024 ~98% 4 KB Good balance for production
3072 100% 12 KB Maximum quality, cost no issue

Fine-Tuning Embeddings for Your Domain

General-purpose models may underperform on domain-specific text (legal, medical, financial). Fine-tuning adapts the model to your vocabulary and semantic relationships.

→

→

Hard negatives matter:

The biggest gains come from mining hard negatives - documents that are semantically similar to the relevant document but don't actually answer the query. Training the model to distinguish these from true positives is what makes fine-tuned embeddings powerful.

05 Vector Databases

A vector database stores your embedding vectors and enables fast approximate nearest neighbor (ANN) search. When a user query comes in, the query embedding is compared against millions of stored vectors to find the most similar chunks.

$$\text{Top-}k = \underset{d \in \mathcal{D}}{\text{argmax-}k}\; \text{sim}(\mathbf{q},\, \mathbf{d}) \quad \text{where } \text{sim} \in \{\cos, \text{dot}, L_2\}$$
Find the k document vectors most similar to the query vector

Why Not Just Use NumPy / Brute Force?

Brute force: \(\mathcal{O}(N \times d)\)

Compare query against every vector. At 10M vectors × 1024 dims, that's ~40 GB of dot products per query. Latency: seconds.

ANN index: \(\mathcal{O}(\log N)\) to \(\mathcal{O}(\sqrt{N})\)

Algorithms like HNSW, IVF, and ScaNN pre-organize vectors into navigable structures. Same 10M vectors, latency: milliseconds.

Key Features to Evaluate

Metadata Filtering

Filter results by attributes (date, author, category) before or after vector search. Critical for multi-tenant apps: "Find relevant docs for this customer only."

Hybrid Search

Combine BM25 (keyword) scores with dense vector scores. Catches exact term matches that embeddings miss. Consistently outperforms either method alone.

Multi-Tenancy

Isolate data between tenants (customers/teams). Options: namespace-based (Pinecone), collection-based (Qdrant), or metadata filtering.

Scalability

How does it perform at 10M, 100M, 1B vectors? Does it support sharding, replication? What's the memory footprint per vector?

Decision framework:
  • Just prototyping? → ChromaDB (in-process, zero setup)
  • Already using Postgres? → pgvector (no new infra)
  • Production, want managed? → Pinecone or Weaviate Cloud
  • Need maximum performance? → Qdrant or Milvus
  • Billion-scale? → Milvus/Zilliz

06 The Retrieval Layer

This is where search meets RAG. Retrieval quality directly determines generation quality. Retrieve bad chunks → get bad answers. No amount of LLM intelligence can fix garbage retrieval.

🗑️

Garbage In, Garbage Out: If you hand a brilliant lawyer the wrong case files, they'll build a compelling but completely wrong argument. The LLM is the lawyer. Your retrieval pipeline is the paralegal who gathers the evidence. If the paralegal is incompetent, the whole case falls apart.

Three Retrieval Paradigms

Paradigm How It Works Strengths Weaknesses
Sparse (BM25) Keyword matching with TF-IDF style scoring Exact match, fast, no ML needed, interpretable Misses synonyms, no semantic understanding
Dense (Embeddings) Cosine similarity between query and doc vectors Semantic matching, handles paraphrases Can miss exact terms, needs embedding model
Hybrid (BM25 + Dense) Run both, merge results via fusion Best of both - exact + semantic More complex, needs score normalization

Reciprocal Rank Fusion (RRF)

$$\text{RRF}(d) = \sum_{r \in \text{rankers}} \frac{1}{k + \text{rank}_r(d)}$$
\(k\) = smoothing constant (typically 60). Documents ranked highly by multiple rankers get boosted.

Re-Ranking with Cross-Encoders

Bi-encoders (embedding models) encode query and document separately - fast but less accurate. Cross-encoders process the query and document together through a transformer - slow but much more accurate.

Bi-Encoder (Stage 1)

Embed query and docs separately. Compare via cosine similarity. Can search millions of docs in milliseconds. Used for initial retrieval.

\(\text{score} = \cos(\text{enc}(q),\, \text{enc}(d))\)

Cross-Encoder (Stage 2)

Feed [query, doc] pair into a single model. Gets full attention between query and doc tokens. Too slow for full corpus, but great for re-ranking top-N.

\(\text{score} = \text{model}([q; d])\)

07 Context Assembly

You've retrieved the top-K chunks. Now you need to assemble them into a prompt that the LLM can effectively use. This step is more nuanced than it appears.

The Prompt Template

System:
You are a helpful assistant. Answer the user's question based ONLY
on the provided context. If the context doesn't contain enough
information, say "I don't have enough information to answer that."
Always cite your sources using [Source N] notation.

Context:
[Source 1] {chunk_1_text}
[Source 2] {chunk_2_text}
[Source 3] {chunk_3_text}
...

User:
{user_question}

Ordering Strategies

Strategy Order When to Use
Relevance-first Most relevant chunk first Default - models attend more to the beginning
Relevance-last Most relevant chunk last Some models attend more to the end (recency bias)
Chronological By document date/position When temporal ordering matters (conversations, logs)
Edges-first Best chunks at start AND end Mitigates "lost in the middle" problem

The "Lost in the Middle" Problem

Research finding (Liu et al., 2023):

LLMs perform significantly worse when the relevant information is buried in the middle of a long context, compared to when it appears at the beginning or end. In experiments, accuracy dropped by up to 20% when the gold passage was in the middle of 20 retrieved passages.

Deduplication

Retrieved chunks may overlap (especially with chunk overlap) or come from the same document section. Before assembling context:

Exact dedup (hash)
→
Near-dedup (similarity > 0.95)
→
Source diversity filter
→
Token budget check

Context Window Management

$$\text{Available tokens} = \text{max\_context} - \text{system\_prompt} - \text{user\_query} - \text{output\_reserve}$$
Calculate how many tokens you can actually fill with retrieved chunks
Practical budget example (GPT-4 Turbo, 128K context):
Max context window 128,000 tokens
System prompt ~500 tokens
User query ~100 tokens
Output reserve ~4,000 tokens
Available for chunks ~123,400 tokens
At 512 tokens/chunk ~240 chunks (but don't - quality degrades!)
Recommended 5–15 chunks (enough for quality without "lost in the middle")

Source Attribution

Each chunk should carry metadata: document title, page number, URL, date. This enables the LLM to produce citations and lets users verify answers.

08 Where the LLM Fits

A critical misconception: the LLM is not replacing search. It's consuming search results. The LLM is a reasoning and synthesis layer that sits on top of the retrieval pipeline.

🧠

Analogy: Think of it as a research team. The retrieval pipeline is the research assistants who gather relevant papers and documents. The LLM is the senior analyst who reads those documents, synthesizes findings, and writes the final report. The analyst doesn't go searching - that's not their job. Their job is to reason over what's been gathered.

The LLM's Role in RAG

What the LLM does:
  • Reads and comprehends retrieved context
  • Synthesizes information across multiple chunks
  • Resolves contradictions between sources
  • Formats the answer appropriately
  • Generates citations/source references
  • Says "I don't know" when context is insufficient
What the LLM does NOT do:
  • Search the knowledge base
  • Decide what's relevant (retrieval does this)
  • Guarantee factual accuracy (it still can hallucinate)
  • Replace the need for good chunking/embedding

Prompt Engineering for RAG

Key prompt design principles:
  1. Ground in context: Explicitly instruct the model to answer only from provided context
  2. Handle unknowns: Tell the model what to do when context doesn't contain the answer
  3. Require citations: Ask the model to reference which source each claim comes from
  4. Set output format: Specify whether you want bullet points, paragraphs, JSON, etc.
  5. Manage confidence: Ask the model to qualify uncertain answers

Handling "I Don't Know"

One of RAG's biggest advantages: the model can admit uncertainty when the retrieved context doesn't support an answer. But this requires careful prompting.

Bad prompt (enables hallucination): 
Answer the user's question.
Context: {chunks}
Question: {query}
Good prompt (controls hallucination): 
Answer ONLY using the context below.
If the context doesn't contain the
answer, respond: "I don't have enough
information in my sources to answer
this question."
Context: {chunks}
Question: {query}

09 Advanced RAG Patterns

Naive RAG (embed → retrieve → generate) works but has limitations. Advanced RAG patterns address specific failure modes through query transformation, adaptive retrieval, and multi-step reasoning.

Query Transformation Techniques

HyDE (Hypothetical Document Embeddings)

Instead of embedding the user's query directly, ask the LLM to generate a hypothetical answer, then embed that and search for similar real documents. The intuition: a hypothetical answer is more semantically similar to real documents than a short question is.

Query
→
LLM generates
hypothetical answer
→
Embed hypothetical
→
Vector search
Multi-Query Retrieval

Generate multiple variations of the user's query using an LLM, retrieve for each variation, then merge results. Captures different facets of the question that a single query might miss.

Example: "How does photosynthesis work?" → Also search: "photosynthesis process steps", "light reactions dark reactions", "chloroplast energy conversion"

Step-Back Prompting

Before retrieving, ask the LLM to identify the higher-level concept behind the user's question. Retrieve for both the original and the "stepped-back" question.

Example: "Why did the 2008 financial crisis happen?" → Step back: "What causes systemic banking crises?"

Adaptive Retrieval Patterns

Self-RAG (Self-Reflective RAG)

The model decides when to retrieve. It generates "reflection tokens" that evaluate whether retrieval is needed, whether retrieved passages are relevant, and whether the generated answer is supported by the context.

Generate initial response
→
Self-check: need retrieval?
→
If yes → retrieve & regenerate
→
Verify answer is supported
Corrective RAG (CRAG)

After retrieval, evaluate the quality of retrieved documents. If confidence is low, trigger web search or other knowledge sources as fallback. Prevents the model from generating answers based on irrelevant retrieved content.

Retrieve docs
→
Grade relevance
→
Correct: use docs
or
Incorrect: web search
Agentic RAG

The LLM acts as an agent with retrieval as a tool. It can decide what to search for, execute multiple searches, reason over intermediate results, and iterate until it has enough information.

Frameworks: LangChain Agents, LlamaIndex Agents, CrewAI, AutoGen

Knowledge Graphs + RAG (GraphRAG)

🕸️

GraphRAG: Instead of (or in addition to) vector search, build a knowledge graph from your documents. Entities become nodes, relationships become edges. At query time, traverse the graph to find relevant entity clusters, then use those as context. Excels at multi-hop reasoning: "What companies has the CEO of Acme Corp's biggest competitor invested in?"

Advanced Patterns Comparison

Pattern Solves Complexity Latency Impact When to Use
HyDE Query-document mismatch Medium +1 LLM call Short queries, keyword-heavy docs
Multi-Query Recall gaps Medium +1 LLM call + N retrievals Ambiguous or broad queries
Step-Back Too-specific queries Low +1 LLM call + 1 retrieval Detailed questions needing background
Self-RAG Unnecessary/harmful retrieval High Variable (0–N retrievals) Mixed queries (some need retrieval, some don't)
CRAG Low-quality retrieval Medium +1 evaluation step Noisy knowledge bases
Agentic RAG Complex multi-step questions High Multiple LLM + retrieval rounds Research tasks, complex queries
GraphRAG Multi-hop reasoning High Graph traversal + LLM Entity-rich domains (legal, biomedical)
Start simple, add complexity only when needed.

Naive RAG with good chunking, a strong embedding model, and a re-ranker will outperform a poorly-implemented "advanced" pattern. Add HyDE or multi-query only after measuring where your retrieval is failing. Use agentic RAG only when the task genuinely requires multi-step reasoning.

10 Evaluation

You can't improve what you can't measure. RAG evaluation is harder than LLM evaluation because you need to assess both the retrieval quality and the generation quality independently.

The RAG Evaluation Triangle

Context Relevance
Are retrieved docs relevant?
Answer Faithfulness
Is the answer grounded?
Answer Relevance
Does it answer the question?
All three must be high for a good RAG system

Key Metrics Explained

Metric What It Measures How to Compute Target
Context Precision % of retrieved chunks that are relevant Relevant retrieved / Total retrieved > 0.7
Context Recall % of relevant chunks that were retrieved Relevant retrieved / Total relevant > 0.8
Faithfulness Are answer claims supported by context? Supported claims / Total claims > 0.9
Answer Relevance Does the answer address the question? Semantic similarity of Q and A > 0.8
Answer Correctness Is the answer factually correct? Comparison to ground truth > 0.85
$$\color{#6c63ff}{\text{Faithfulness}} = \frac{|\text{claims supported by context}|}{|\text{total claims in answer}|}$$
The most critical RAG metric - measures hallucination rate

Evaluation Frameworks

RAGAS

Open-source framework for automated RAG evaluation. Computes faithfulness, answer relevance, context precision, and context recall using an LLM as judge. No ground truth labels needed for most metrics. 

from ragas import evaluate
from ragas.metrics import (
    faithfulness,
    answer_relevancy,
    context_precision,
    context_recall
)

result = evaluate(
    dataset,
    metrics=[faithfulness,
             answer_relevancy,
             context_precision,
             context_recall]
)
TruLens

Evaluation + observability platform. Traces every step of your RAG pipeline and computes quality metrics. Supports custom feedback functions and multiple LLM providers as evaluators.

from trulens_eval import (
    Feedback, TruLlama
)
from trulens_eval.feedback import (
    Groundedness
)

grounded = Groundedness(
    groundedness_provider=openai
)
f_groundedness = Feedback(
    grounded.groundedness_measure
).on(context).on_output()

Practical evaluation workflow:
  1. Create a golden test set - 50–200 question-answer pairs with source documents marked
  2. Automate with RAGAS - Run on every change to chunking, embedding model, or prompt
  3. Track metrics over time - Dashboard showing faithfulness, relevance, and retrieval quality trends
  4. Sample-check with humans - Randomly audit 10% of automated "pass" results to calibrate
  5. Failure analysis - For every failure, determine: was it a retrieval failure or a generation failure? Fix the right component.
The most important debugging question:

When your RAG system gives a wrong answer, always ask: "Were the right chunks retrieved?" If yes → it's a generation/prompt problem. If no → it's a retrieval problem (chunking, embedding, or search). In practice, ~80% of RAG failures are retrieval failures.
