01 The Jaguar Problem

Type "jaguar" into a search engine. What do you mean? The same query has at least four completely distinct interpretations — and the search engine doesn't know which one you want.

Jaguar (Car)

Luxury British automaker. F-Type, XJ, E-Pace. "Jaguar dealer near me." Most popular interpretation in many markets.

Jaguar (Animal)

Panthera onca. Third-largest big cat. Amazon rainforest apex predator. Popular for nature enthusiasts and students.

Jaguar (NFL Team)

Jacksonville Jaguars. AFC South division. "Jaguars game schedule 2025." Spikes during football season.

Jaguar (macOS)

Mac OS X 10.2 Jaguar. Apple's 2002 operating system release. Niche but still searched by tech historians.

🎯

Analogy: Imagine ordering "chips" in a global restaurant. In the US you'd get potato chips, in the UK you'd get french fries, and in a casino you'd get poker chips. The word is the same — the intent is ambiguous. A good search engine hedges its bets by covering all plausible intents.

Ambiguity Is the Norm

Research from TREC and Microsoft shows that over 30% of web queries are ambiguous — they have multiple valid interpretations. For these queries, a pure relevance ranking is actually a bad strategy, because it bets everything on a single interpretation.

02 Why Diversity?

When a query is ambiguous, showing 10 results from the most popular interpretation fails users with minority intents. Diversity ensures coverage across all plausible meanings.

Without Diversity

Query "apple": 10 results about Apple Inc. A user looking for apple recipes is completely unserved. They have to reformulate their query, adding friction.

With Diversity

Query "apple": 6 Apple Inc., 2 apple recipes, 1 apple nutrition, 1 Apple Records. Everyone finds something relevant within the first few results.

The Diversity Principle

Diversity doesn't mean sacrificing relevance — it means maximizing the probability that at least one result satisfies the user's actual intent, whatever it may be. It's a form of risk management for ambiguous queries.

The Portfolio Theory Connection

Diversity in search is mathematically analogous to portfolio diversification in finance. Just as a diversified investment portfolio reduces risk by spreading across sectors, a diverse search result page reduces the risk of completely missing the user's intent.

$$P(\text{user satisfied}) = 1 - \prod_{i=1}^{k} (1 - P(\text{result}_i \text{ matches intent}))$$
With diverse results, the probability of at least one hit increases multiplicatively
When NOT to Diversify

Navigational queries ("gmail login", "amazon.com") have a single clear intent — diversifying these would be counterproductive. Diversity is most valuable for informational and ambiguous queries.

04 MMR — Maximal Marginal Relevance

MMR is the standard algorithm for balancing relevance and diversity. At each step, it selects the document that maximizes a weighted combination of relevance to the query and novelty relative to already-selected documents.

$$\text{MMR}(d) = \lambda \cdot \text{Sim}(d, q) - (1 - \lambda) \cdot \max_{d_j \in S} \text{Sim}(d, d_j)$$
λ=1 → pure relevance  |  λ=0 → maximum diversity

How MMR Works Step by Step

  1. Initialize: Start with an empty selected set S and all candidates in C
  2. First pick: Select the most relevant document (greedy, same as pure relevance)
  3. For each subsequent slot:
    • For each remaining candidate d in C, compute relevance to query: \(\text{Sim}(d, q)\)
    • Compute max similarity to any already-selected doc: \(\max_{d_j \in S} \text{Sim}(d, d_j)\)
    • Compute MMR score: \(\lambda \cdot \text{rel} - (1-\lambda) \cdot \text{max\_sim}\)
  4. Select the candidate with the highest MMR score, add to S, remove from C
  5. Repeat until you have enough results

The λ Knob

λ = 1.0: Pure relevance ranking (no diversity consideration at all).
λ = 0.5: Balanced trade-off — good default for ambiguous queries.
λ = 0.0: Maximum diversity — each new result is as different as possible from what's already shown.

Similarity Function

The similarity between documents can be computed via:
• Category match (same topic = high sim)
• Embedding cosine (semantic similarity)
• Snippet overlap (textual similarity)
The choice of similarity function shapes what "diversity" means.

MMR Complexity

$$O(k \times n) \text{ where } k = \text{results to select}, \; n = \text{candidate pool size}$$
Linear in both outputs and candidates — efficient for typical search result pages

06 Category Diversification

In e-commerce, diversity means spreading results across brands, price ranges, and product categories — not just topic intents. Users searching for "running shoes" don't want 10 Nike models; they want to compare across brands.

DimensionWithout DiversityWith Diversity
Brand 10 Nike shoes 3 Nike, 3 Adidas, 2 New Balance, 2 Puma
Price All $100-120 Spread: $50, $100, $150, $200 ranges
Category All running shoes Running, casual, training, hiking
Rating All 4.5+ stars Mix of highly-rated and popular new arrivals
Seller All from same seller Mix of sellers for competitive pricing
Stripe-Based Diversification

A simple approach: round-robin across categories. Position 1 = top from category A, position 2 = top from category B, etc. Not mathematically optimal like MMR, but easy to implement, debug, and explain to stakeholders.

Multi-Dimensional Diversity

Real e-commerce search diversifies across multiple dimensions simultaneously. You want brand diversity AND price diversity AND category diversity. This is harder than single-dimension MMR — it requires multi-objective optimization.

Greedy Round-Robin

Simple and fast. Cycle through categories, picking the best remaining from each. Works well for 2-3 dimensions. O(k × n) complexity.

Multi-MMR

Extend MMR with multiple similarity functions (one per dimension). Weight each dimension. More principled but harder to tune and explain.

07 Result Deduplication

Before diversifying, remove near-duplicate results — multiple pages with essentially the same content waste precious result slots and frustrate users.

Deduplication Techniques

URL Dedup

Normalize URLs (strip tracking params, www prefix, trailing slashes). Cheapest check — catches exact duplicates and tracking-parameter variants.

Cost: O(1) per doc  |  Catches: ~30% of dupes

Content Fingerprinting

SimHash or MinHash on page content. Catches pages with same text but different URLs (syndicated articles, content farms, mirror sites).

Cost: O(n) build, O(1) compare  |  Catches: ~60% of dupes

Snippet Dedup

Compare extracted snippets. If two results would show identical text in the SERP, collapse them — users would see redundant content anyway.

Cost: O(n²) naive  |  Catches: ~20% of dupes

Semantic Dedup

Embedding cosine similarity > 0.95 = near-duplicate. Most expensive but catches paraphrased content and translations.

Cost: O(n²) or ANN  |  Catches: ~90% of dupes

$$\text{SimHash}(d_1, d_2) = \frac{\text{matching\_bits}}{\text{total\_bits}} > \theta \implies \text{duplicate}$$
SimHash: O(1) comparison after O(n) fingerprint computation. Typically θ ≥ 0.95

Host Collapsing

A related technique: host collapsing limits results from any single domain to 2-3 entries. This prevents one authoritative site from dominating the entire first page, even if all its pages are technically unique.

08 Measuring Diversity

How do you know if your diversification is working? Standard metrics capture different facets of diversity.

MetricWhat It MeasuresRangeHigher = ?
α-NDCG Relevance with diminishing returns per intent — repeated intents are penalized [0, 1] Better
Intent Coverage Fraction of known intents represented in top-k results [0, 1] Better
Subtopic Recall How many distinct subtopics appear at least once in top-k [0, 1] Better
ILS (Intra-List Similarity) Average pairwise similarity of results — measures homogeneity [0, 1] Less diverse
ERR-IA Expected Reciprocal Rank, intent-aware — probability user is satisfied at each position [0, 1] Better
$$\alpha\text{-NDCG} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{k} \frac{J(d_i, \theta) \cdot (1 - \alpha)^{c_{i-1}(\theta)}}{\log_2(i+1)}}{\text{Ideal}_\alpha}$$
α-NDCG: penalizes redundant coverage of the same intent (α typically 0.5)

Reading the Formula

  • \(J(d_i, \theta)\): Does document \(d_i\) cover intent \(\theta\)? (0 or 1)
  • \(c_{i-1}(\theta)\): How many documents above position \(i\) already cover intent \(\theta\)?
  • \((1-\alpha)^{c_{i-1}}\): Novelty discount — each additional document covering the same intent is worth less
  • When \(\alpha = 0\), this reduces to standard NDCG (no diversity reward)
  • When \(\alpha = 1\), only the first document per intent counts (maximum diversity reward)
Key Insight

Standard NDCG rewards relevance without considering diversity. α-NDCG adds a novelty discount: the second result about "jaguar cars" is worth less than the first result about "jaguar animals," because the car intent is already covered. This aligns the metric with the user's actual experience — once an intent is satisfied, additional results for that intent have diminishing value.

Metric Comparison

Offline Metrics

α-NDCG, ERR-IA, and subtopic recall are computed on labeled datasets where each result is tagged with its intent. Useful for algorithm development and regression testing.

Online Metrics

In production, measure query reformulation rate (lower = more diverse), pogo-sticking (bouncing between results = not diverse enough), and abandonment rate (users giving up).

09 Key Takeaway

Diversity is a ranking objective, not an afterthought

For ambiguous queries, relevance alone produces redundant results that fail minority-intent users. MMR, intent-aware diversification, and category balancing ensure that your search results serve the broadest possible set of user intents. The goal: every user finds what they need within the first page — even if the search engine can't know which interpretation they meant.

Final Thought

The best search results feel obvious in hindsight — "of course the first page should cover cars, animals, and the NFL team." But achieving that coverage requires deliberate algorithmic effort. Without diversity mechanisms, even the most sophisticated relevance model will produce a monotonous, single-intent result page. Diversity is what transforms a good search engine into a great one.
